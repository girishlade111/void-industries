WHY WE KILLED
OUR MOODBOARDS.

They felt responsible. They weren't. Here's what we replaced them with.

BY MARA KLINE 8 MIN READ SEP 12, 2026

Every studio has a phase where the moodboard is the deliverable. Forty images of concrete textures, three screenshots of Helvetica posters, a photo of a pigeon against a brutalist facade. Everyone nods. Someone says "energy." The project continues exactly as it would have anyway.

We had that phase. Then we killed it.

THE PROBLEM WITH VIBES

Moodboards communicate mood. That's literally the name. But mood is the least actionable thing you can hand a team. "Make it feel like this collage" produces four months of interpretive dance — every designer on the project resolves the vibe differently, and the system fractures before the first component ships.

Worse: moodboards make clients feel decisions have been made when nothing has. Approving a moodboard is cheap dopamine. Approving a type scale with actual optical sizes and fallbacks is real commitment. We were front-loading the cheap part and pretending it was strategy.

If the artifact can't be argued with precisely, it can't move the project forward.

WHAT REPLACED IT

We swapped moodboards for three artifacts, produced in the first week instead of the second:

  • A constraint sheet. Not "what it should feel like" — what it cannot be. No rounded corners. No more than two typefaces. One accent color, never gradients. Clients argue with constraints productively; they nod at vibes passively.
  • A wired, ugly prototype. One page, real content, real hierarchy, zero polish. Interactive from day one. If a stakeholder can click it, they'll tell you the truth. If they can only look at it, they'll tell you what they think you want to hear.
  • A reference teardown. Two existing products or brands — not aspirational mood images — with annotations on what specifically works and what we'd steal. Concrete beats atmosphere every time.

WHAT WE KEPT

We didn't ban imagery. We banned unaccountable imagery. Every visual reference now ships with a sentence explaining the mechanical decision it implies: "this photography style" becomes "hard flash, shallow depth, subjects centered — enforced in the shoot brief." The vibe becomes a rule. Rules survive handoffs; vibes don't.

The first project we ran this way was Signal Decay. The constraint sheet alone killed two weeks of circular feedback. The identity that shipped had more conviction than anything we'd produced with a wall of textures behind it — and the client could articulate exactly why, because they'd argued with the constraints, not just approved the collage.

THE HONEST CAVEAT

Moodboards still exist in our process — behind closed doors, early, cheap, disposable. They're a sketching tool for us, not a presentation tool for clients. The moment they become the artifact you're selling, you've outsourced your judgment to a Pinterest board.

We still say "energy" in kickoffs. We just don't bill for it.
